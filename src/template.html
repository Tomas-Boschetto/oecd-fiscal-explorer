Categories

Spending follows the UN Classification of the Functions of Government (COFOG), which every country here uses, so “health” or “education” mean the same thing everywhere. Each category below lists the subcategories you can drill into. In the main spending view, environmental protection, housing and community amenities, and recreation and culture are shown together as one bar. Revenue follows the national-accounts categories.

Spending categories

Revenue sources

Balance

Revenue minus spending, also called net lending (+) or net borrowing (−). A negative balance is a deficit that has to be financed by borrowing or by running down assets. It applies to the government as a whole and can't be split by category.

Debt

Debt is a stock: what is owed at the end of a year, not what is spent or collected during it. A deficit adds to government debt; a surplus can reduce it. The Debt view splits what a country owes by who borrowed. Private debt is often larger than government debt, and both matter for financial stability.

Government

Gross debt of general government: bonds, bills and loans owed by central, state and local government and social security funds. Japan's government debt is about 240% of GDP, Germany's about 63%. A large share is often owed to the country's own residents and central bank.

Not included: debt of public companies (for example state-owned utilities or railways run as companies) and future pension promises. Assets are not subtracted, so this is gross, not net, debt.

Households

Loans and debt securities owed by households, mostly mortgages, plus consumer credit, car loans, credit cards and student loans. High where home ownership is financed with long mortgages, as in Switzerland, Australia, the Netherlands or the Nordic countries; very low in Argentina, where little credit is available.

Not included: unpaid bills and informal loans between people.

Non-financial companies

Bank loans and bonds owed by businesses outside the financial sector: manufacturers, retailers, energy and property companies. Countries hosting holding companies or multinational treasuries (Luxembourg, Ireland, the Netherlands) show very high figures that reflect cross-border finance more than domestic borrowing.

Not included: debt of banks and other financial companies, trade credit between firms, and equity.

About the data

This explorer compares what governments spend, what they collect and the resulting deficit or surplus across OECD countries and the large non-OECD G20 economies (Argentina, Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa), from 1995 to 2024. All figures cover general government: central, state and local government plus social security funds, consolidated, on the national-accounts basis (SNA 2008). That is what makes countries comparable, whatever their federal structure.

How to use it

Single year / PeriodPick one year from the list, or a range of years.
Unit% of GDP compares the size of government across countries. US$ (PPP) converts with purchasing-power parities, which suits comparing real effort. US$ (exchange rates) reflects market value. For a period, US$ figures are added up over the years; % of GDP is the share of the period's combined GDP (a GDP-weighted average).
Break downSpending by category, revenue by source, or debt by sector (government, households, companies).
DetailMain categories, or the subcategories of one of them. Clicking a coloured segment does the same; ← All categories goes back. See the Categories tab for what each one contains.
Sort byTotal, the comparison total, balance, name, or any single category. The category you sort by is drawn first, from the zero line, so it lines up across countries.
CategoriesTurn categories on and off. The total and the thin bar keep using the full figures.
CountriesPick countries one by one or by group. Other non-OECD economies and EU / euro-area totals can be added.
Complete data onlyHides countries missing any year of the chosen period, and in subcategory views countries without that breakdown. In the main views, countries without a breakdown show a grey bar. Those left out are listed under the chart.
Table / Copy as CSVSee or copy the exact numbers behind the current view.

Reading the chart

In the main views, the thick bar is the side you're breaking down and the thin dark bar under it is the other side: revenue under spending, spending under revenue. The right-hand column is the balance, revenue minus spending; bars to the left are deficits, bars to the right surpluses. The first row sums up all countries shown (GDP-weighted in % of GDP, a total in US$).

Subcategory views show only the breakdown, with the category total at the end of each bar. The balance is left out there because a deficit can't be assigned to particular categories: government revenue is pooled.

Method and comparability

  • Totals and main categories come from OECD National Accounts at a Glance, chapter 6. Revenue minus spending equals the published balance for every country and year.
  • Spending subcategories come from OECD Table 11 (COFOG level 2), available for 37 OECD countries. Where a country's subcategories don't add up exactly to its category total, they are scaled in proportion (usually by under 0.5 points of GDP, at most 2.4). The same applies to the categories against total spending.
  • Revenue subcategories come from OECD Table 12. "Other revenue" is total revenue minus taxes and social contributions.
  • Grey bars mean the country has no breakdown for that view and year.
  • Debt comes from the IMF Global Debt Database, in % of GDP: general government gross debt, and household and non-financial corporate debt (loans and debt securities). Debt is a stock, so a period shows the level at its end and the change since its start, not a sum or average. In US$, debt is converted with the same year's GDP. Government debt here is the IMF's measure and can differ from national or EU (Maastricht) figures, mainly in how debt is valued and which liabilities are counted. It also differs from the OECD's national-accounts debt used elsewhere in the dataset.
  • Coverage. Most OECD countries have complete data from 1995. Recent years are missing for some (for example Japan, Canada, New Zealand), and Türkiye and Australia lack revenue detail.

Estimated figures (marked “est.”)

  • United States, spending subcategories. The US reports no subcategories to the OECD. Category totals are the OECD figures; only the split inside them is estimated. Health is split with the CMS National Health Expenditure Accounts (government-funded spending only: Medicare, Medicaid, CHIP, veterans' and military health care, other public programmes, public health, public research and investment). Other categories use BEA government spending by function (NIPA table 3.16), which has fewer lines: primary and secondary education are one line (shown under 9.1), and defence, environment, housing and culture have no split.
  • Personal vs corporate income tax. Where the national accounts don't split them (about half the countries, including the US; some report zeros instead of empty cells), the split comes from OECD Revenue Statistics (codes 1100, 1200, 1300) and is applied to the national-accounts total.
  • General consumption taxes. National accounts count only VAT as VAT and put retail sales taxes (for example US state sales taxes) with excise duties. For comparability, sales and other general turnover taxes (Revenue Statistics 5112 and 5113) are moved next to VAT. Revenue Statistics lags by a year or two, so its latest ratio is carried forward for up to two years.
  • Government debt for Mexico, New Zealand and Argentina is missing from the Global Debt Database and comes from the IMF World Economic Outlook instead (general government gross debt).
  • China, India, Brazil, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and South Africa are not (or only partly) in the OECD national accounts, so all their figures are estimates:
    • Totals (revenue, spending, balance, debt) and GDP in US$: IMF Fiscal Monitor and World Economic Outlook, general government, to 2024.
    • Spending by category: IMF Government Finance Statistics (COFOG), general government, scaled to the IMF spending total. Available for Brazil (from 2010), South Africa (from 2000), China (from 2005) and Indonesia (2008–2023, main categories only). China reports subcategories only for some categories. India and Saudi Arabia publish no breakdown by function for general government, so they show totals only.
    • Taxes: OECD Global Revenue Statistics for Brazil, China, Indonesia and South Africa, mapped the same way as for Argentina. India and Saudi Arabia have no tax breakdown; Saudi revenue comes mostly from oil.
    • Brazil's figures differ from earlier versions of this explorer, which used partial OECD data up to 2021.
  • Argentina is not in the OECD national accounts, so all its figures are estimates built from other sources:
    • Totals (revenue, spending, balance, debt) and GDP in US$: IMF Fiscal Monitor and World Economic Outlook, general government, to 2024. Later IMF years are projections and are left out.
    • Spending by category: Ministry of Economy, Gasto Público Consolidado (nation, provinces and municipalities), to 2023. Its total is higher than the IMF's (41.9% vs 37.3% of GDP in 2023, a difference in coverage), so only its shares are used, scaled to the IMF total.
    • Defence: SIPRI military expenditure (via the World Bank), because the Argentine series combines defence and security; the rest counts as police and public order.
    • Taxes: OECD Revenue Statistics in Latin America. The provincial turnover tax (Ingresos Brutos) counts as a general consumption tax; export duties and the tax on bank transactions as other taxes on products.
    • Health has no subcategories, water and sewerage are shown under water supply, municipal urban services under waste management, and other revenue has no breakdown.
