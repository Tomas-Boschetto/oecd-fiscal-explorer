How to read it. The thick bar is the part you're breaking down (spending by function, or revenue by source). The thin dark bar under it is the other side (revenue, or spending). The column on the right is the balance, revenue minus spending: bars to the left are deficits, bars to the right surpluses.

Subcategories. Use Detail, or click a coloured segment, to split any spending category into its COFOG subcategories (for example Health into hospitals, outpatient care, medical products, public health, R&D and other). Subcategory views show only the breakdown, with each bar's total at its end; the balance is left out because a deficit can't be assigned to particular categories. Subcategories come from the OECD's detailed COFOG table and cover 37 countries. The United States doesn't report them, so its split is estimated (marked “est.”): health from the CMS National Health Expenditure Accounts (government-funded spending by type of service), the other categories from BEA government spending by function (NIPA table 3.16). The US totals per category stay the OECD figures; only the split is estimated, and BEA covers fewer lines (for example primary and secondary schooling are one line, shown under 9.1; defence, environment, housing and culture have no split).

Revenue subcategories. Under Revenue, use Detail or click a segment to split a source: income taxes into personal, corporate and other; taxes on production into general consumption taxes (VAT plus retail sales taxes, so the US and Canada compare fairly with VAT countries), excise and customs, and other production taxes; social contributions by who pays; other revenue into sales and fees, interest, other property income, transfers and capital taxes. All come from the OECD government accounts (Table 12). Where those accounts don't split personal and corporate income tax (about half the countries, including the US), the split comes from OECD Revenue Statistics and is marked “est.”. The US has no VAT; its state and local sales taxes are counted with VAT under general consumption taxes, sized from OECD Revenue Statistics (marked “est.”).

Periods. For a period, US$ figures are added up over all its years. % of GDP figures are the share of the period's combined GDP (a GDP-weighted average), so large and small years count in proportion.

Comparability. All figures are general government (central, state, local and social security together), on the SNA 2008 basis. The breakdown by function (COFOG) sometimes doesn't add up exactly to total spending; the gap is spread across the categories in proportion (at most 2.4 points of GDP, usually under 0.5). "Other revenue" covers capital taxes, sales of goods and services, property income (e.g. Norway's oil fund returns) and transfers. Debt interest sits inside "General public services". Countries with no breakdown for a year show a grey bar.

Coverage. Most OECD countries have complete data from 1995; Japan, Canada and some others end earlier than 2024. US$ PPP uses purchasing-power parities, which suits comparing real effort; exchange-rate US$ reflects market value.
